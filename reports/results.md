# Results: do high-quality firms outperform low-quality firms?

Generated by `scripts/run_backtest.py` on 2026-10-03. Return months 2012-02 to 2026-09 (176 months); about 287 firms per formation date in the composite sort (current S&P 500 members, ex financials and utilities). Portfolios are formed at each month end from the latest 10-K filed at least one trading day earlier and held for one month. Returns are total returns (Yahoo adjusted close), in % per month.

## Short answer

4 of 14 long-short tests (signals x EW/VW) have |t| >= 1.96, 1 of them with the hypothesised sign (high quality beats low). At a 5% level about 0.7 would clear that bar by chance alone, and Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue for t > 3 after decades of factor mining. With this sample, a long-short needs a mean of roughly +0.46%/month (+5.5% a year) to reach t = 1.96, so small effects cannot be told apart from zero.

- **Piotroski F (groups)**: Q5-Q1 -0.81%/month EW (t = -2.42), -0.59% VW (t = -1.57): significant EW (LOW quality beat high quality).
- **Piotroski F (quintiles)**: Q5-Q1 -0.42%/month EW (t = -2.24), -0.52% VW (t = -2.27): significant EW (LOW quality beat high quality); significant VW (LOW quality beat high quality).

## Reading the evidence

- **Piotroski F (groups); Piotroski F (quintiles): significant with the opposite sign to the hypothesis.** This is the direction survivorship bias predicts. A firm in today's S&P 500 that scored badly years ago is, by construction, one that survived and recovered, so its later returns are high; the low scorers that failed are not in the data. Treat this as a property of the sample, not as evidence that low quality earns a premium, until it is repeated on a point-in-time universe that includes delisted firms.
  - Piotroski F (groups), bottom bucket: the largest contributors are BE, CVNA, AMD, MU, NFLX. Without them its mean falls from +2.27% to +1.95% a month, so the effect is not just a few names.
  - Piotroski F (quintiles), bottom bucket: the largest contributors are BE, TSLA, MRNA, SMCI, NFLX. Without them its mean falls from +1.88% to +1.71% a month, so the effect is not just a few names.
- **Accruals (cash flow): significant equal-weighted only.** The spread sits in the smaller members of the index; in value-weighted portfolios, dominated by the largest firms, it cannot be told apart from zero.
- **Altman Z; Beneish M; Composite; Accruals (balance sheet): no detectable effect** in either weighting.
- No signal's bucket means rise steadily from Q1 to Q5 in either weighting.
- The 3/6/12-month buy-and-hold t-stats use overlapping returns. Newey-West errors are known to over-reject in samples this short at long horizons (Ang and Bekaert 2007, Review of Financial Studies 20, 651-707), so read the 12-month t-stats as optimistic.

## Why the results may be weak, and what they cannot show

- **Large caps only.** Accounting anomalies are strongest in small, illiquid, less-followed stocks (Fama and French 2008, Dissecting Anomalies; Hou, Xue and Zhang 2020, Replicating Anomalies). S&P 500 firms are the most heavily analysed stocks in the world, so mispricing should be small here.
- **Survivorship bias.** The universe is today's S&P 500 list. Firms that failed, were acquired cheaply or dropped out of the index since 2012 are missing, and those are disproportionately the low-quality firms. Leaving them out flatters Q1, which pushes Q5-Q1 toward zero or below. Firms that joined the index because they grew are in the sample from 2012, which selects on later success in every bucket. No delisting returns are included (Shumway 1997). The bias is visible: over the same months the equal-weighted average of these firms returned +1.55%/month against +1.23% for SPY.
- **Publication and crowding.** All four signals were published between 1968 and 2000. McLean and Pontiff (2016) find anomaly returns fall by about a third to a half after publication as investors trade on them.
- **Short sample.** About fourteen years of monthly data from one market regime. Several sub-period estimates rest on 80 or so months.
- **Signals built for other jobs.** Altman Z and Beneish M were designed to predict bankruptcy and earnings manipulation, not returns; most S&P 500 firms are far from both thresholds, so their quintiles mostly sort ordinary firms.
- **No trading costs, no shorting costs.** A monthly-rebalanced long-short pays both.

## Data notes

- Risk-free rate and factors: Kenneth French data library (RF, Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA, MOM).
- 6.5% of holdings have no market cap (mostly multi-class firms whose share count is not in companyfacts); they are equal-weighted but left out of value weights.
- Stocks that stop trading are valued at their last price for the rest of the period (no delisting returns from Yahoo); see the run log for any such cases.
- Parameters (15-month staleness, quintiles, 6 Newey-West lags, the F group cutoffs) were fixed before looking at returns and are not tuned.
